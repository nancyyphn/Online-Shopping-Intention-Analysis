Executive Summary

This analysis compares on-site behavior between visitors who completed a purchase and those who did not, across five session-level metrics: engagement depth (ProductRelated pages, duration), friction (bounce rate, exit rate), and intent signal strength (page value). The goal is to identify which behaviors most reliably distinguish converting sessions, so that product, UX, and marketing teams can prioritize the highest-leverage improvements to conversion rate.

Reading the chart

Why a Gap Index, not raw %

Metrics have different units and directions (rates should fall, engagement should rise). Normalizing each metric to its own 0–100 scale makes all five directly comparable on one axis.

Gap = 100 − (min(np, p) ÷ max(np, p) × 100)

Top driver

Page Value separates the two segments more than any other metric — non-purchasers sit at just 7% of the purchasing segment's average.

Friction matters more than breadth

Bounce and exit rate outrank both engagement-depth metrics, suggesting reducing early drop-off has more leverage than encouraging more page views.

03

Key Business Insights

01

Page Value is the strongest purchase signal

At 13.8× higher for purchasers, Page Value shows by far the widest gap of any metric — visitors who reach high-value pages are overwhelmingly more likely to convert.

02

Friction suppresses conversion more than depth drives it

Bounce Rate (66.7) and Exit Rate (60.0) score higher on the Gap Index than either engagement metric, meaning removing early drop-off may have more leverage than adding content.

03

Time-on-site tracks conversion, not just page count

ProductRelated Duration rose 75.4% for purchasers, slightly ahead of page-count growth (67.9%) — depth of engagement matters as much as breadth.

04

Purchasers browse ~1.7× more product pages

Purchasing sessions average 48.2 ProductRelated pages vs 28.7 for non-purchasers, consistent with deliberate comparison-shopping behavior before checkout.

05

No single metric acts alone

The pattern across all five metrics is consistent: purchasers are more engaged and encounter less friction — reinforcing that conversion is driven by a combined behavioral profile, not one variable.
